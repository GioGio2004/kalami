# Kalami — where things stand (4 October 2026)

This is the plain-language version of what is happening with Kalami right now. The technical audit with all the evidence is in `AUDIT-2026-10-04.md`; this document explains the same picture in normal words, so you can decide what to do next.

---

## 1. The one-paragraph summary

Kalami works. Students can sign up, join a course with a code, take quizzes, midterms and finals on a phone or a laptop, and do HTML/CSS tasks in the sandbox on a laptop. Lecturers can build all of that in the studio or through their AI assistant, publish it, watch students work live, and grade. The security foundation is solid: nobody can see another person's data, answer keys never reach students early by accident of permissions, and nothing a student types can run code. What is **not** ready is a handful of exam-logic details that would hurt a real graded exam (four of them, about two days of work), and the "boring" operations side: no automatic backups, no automated tests on push, no limits against abuse. Costs are not a problem at your size; two platform limits are, and both are explained below. Verdict: run a low-stakes pilot with your own class after the four fixes; let colleagues in after two or three more weeks of hardening; don't put graded university exams of hundreds of students on it until a real load test has been done.

## 2. What Kalami is made of today

There are two websites and one backend:

| Piece | Address | Who uses it | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Student app | app.kalami.space (code in the `kalami` folder) | students | dashboard, courses, quizzes, code tasks, notification bell |
| Staff app ("Kalami AntiCheat") | staff.kalami.space (code in the `kalami-stuff` folder) | lecturers, university admins, you | the studio, grading, invites, the AI connector |
| Backend (Convex) | one database in the EU | both apps | every rule, every piece of data, the grading, the timers |
| Sign-in (Clerk) | — | everyone | accounts, passwords, "Sign in with Kalami" for AI assistants |
| Hosting (Vercel) | — | — | serves the two websites |

Important thing to understand: **the websites are "thin"**. When a student answers a question, the browser talks directly to the Convex backend; Vercel is not involved. That is why Vercel costs are basically zero and why all the capacity questions are about Convex and Clerk.

The AI connector (MCP) lets a lecturer's own assistant (Claude, ChatGPT, Cursor…) create courses, quizzes and code tasks **as drafts**. Agents cannot publish, cannot delete, and cannot see students or grades. That is enforced by the backend, not just by instructions — I verified it.

## 3. What works today (verified)

Everything in this list was traced in the code and most of it is covered by the 94 automated backend tests, which all pass:

* **Accounts**: sign-up, onboarding with the honesty notice (Georgian and English), the rule that an account is either a student or staff, never both. You are the only exception (super admin).
* **Universities and invites**: admins invite lecturers by email with a link that expires in 14 days, works once, and only for the invited address.
* **Courses and joining**: join codes, students join only published courses of their own university, wrong-code guessing is throttled.
* **Quizzes, midterms, finals**: single choice, multiple choice, short answer, essay; shuffled per student; time limits; attempt limits; automatic grading; essays graded by hand; best attempt counts.
* **Code tasks**: step-by-step HTML/CSS tasks with live ticks, hidden checks on submit, a different variant per student, red-pen comments from the lecturer. A task cannot be saved unless its own solution passes every check.
* **Integrity counters**: tab switches, time away, fullscreen exits, blocked paste/copy, second tab, with a green/yellow/red colour for the lecturer.
* **AI connector**: 12 tools, drafts only, every action logged with "done by agent".
* **Notifications** (new this week): the bell in the student app, "new work" when something is published, reminders 24 hours and 1 hour before a deadline.
* **Mobile**: the student app works on phones; code tasks are blocked on phones on purpose; strict exams need fullscreen.

## 4. What does not exist yet

These are mentioned in the plans or in the marketing text but are **not built**. I checked each one in the code:

* ImageKit — only a rule that task images must come from an ImageKit address. No upload, no account connection. And that rule currently accepts *any* ImageKit account because the setting that pins it to yours was never set.
* YouTube embeds, lectures/modules/lessons, surveys.
* Email of any kind (no announcements, no invite emails — admins copy a link by hand).
* PWA (install on home screen), push notifications. The bell works inside the app only.
* Group linking (lecturer group ↔ student group). Joining is by code only.
* File uploads, analytics, error reporting, dark mode.
* Two-factor sign-in for staff. The plan says "required"; the code doesn't enforce it. It may be a setting in Clerk — only you can check.
* Backups, automated tests on every push, a "don't deploy during an exam" guard, data retention/deletion. None of these exist.

## 6. What the audit found, in plain words

I checked the code the way an attacker, a careless student, a nosy lecturer and a tired exam supervisor would. Five separate reviews looked at permissions, the AI connector, exam behaviour, the sandbox, and the operations side.

### 6.1 The good news first

* **Nobody can reach data they shouldn't.** Every backend function checks who you are and what you own. A lecturer from university A cannot even find out that a course at university B exists. Students only see their own work. I found no gaps.
* **Answer keys are stored separately** and never go to a student's browser except when results are supposed to be visible (see the first problem below for the one exception that is a *logic* mistake, not a permissions mistake).
* **Student HTML/CSS cannot run code.** The preview is a locked frame with scripts off. Lecturer text cannot inject HTML either.
* **The AI connector is safe**: sign-in per lecturer, drafts only, nothing about students, everything logged.
* **The platform basics are fine**: no known vulnerable dependencies, both apps build, all tests pass, no secrets leak to browsers, the Clerk webhook is signature-checked.

### 6.2 Four things that would hurt a real exam

These are the ones to fix before any graded quiz. All four are confirmed in the code; none is hard.

**1. Students get the answer key as soon as they submit — if the quiz has no closing time.**
The default quiz settings are "show full results after close" with no closing time set. The code treats "no closing time" as "closed already", so the moment a student submits — even a blank attempt — they see the correct answers. They can share them with friends who haven't started, or, if you allowed two attempts, retake with the key and keep the better score.
*Until fixed*: always set a closing time on every quiz, or set results to "score only".

**2. If one answer fails to save, the other answers waiting with it are silently lost — and Submit still goes through.**
Answers are saved in small groups. If the first one in a group is refused by the server (for example because you edited that question during the exam), the rest of the group is thrown away. The student's screen still shows those questions as "answered", and pressing Submit grades the exam without them. The only warning is a small red "couldn't save" text.

**3. Editing a published quiz while students are taking it breaks their saved answers.**
The studio lets you edit, delete or reorder questions on a published quiz. Editing an option regenerates its internal id, so every saved answer to that question becomes "nothing selected" and scores 0. Reordering reshuffles everybody's question order mid-exam. Deleting leaves counters like "5 of 4 answered". Nothing re-grades afterwards. (The AI assistant is already blocked from this; the website isn't.)
*Until fixed*: never touch a published quiz after the first student has started.

**4. Quizzes with a closing time have no grace period.**
Timed exams accept saves for 15 seconds after the time runs out, which covers the last keystroke and a slow network. Quizzes that end at a fixed clock time don't: the last answer of every student still typing at the bell is refused, and they're marked "submitted automatically" as if they were late. One-line fix.

### 6.3 Two more "high" items that are about operations, not exam logic

**5. Nothing protects the project itself.** No automatic backup of the database. No automated tests when you push. The student app's copy of the backend types and the shared sandbox code are synced by hand — forget once and the student app breaks silently. No guard against deploying in the middle of an exam. The honesty notice promises students their data is deleted after an agreed period; nothing does that.

**6. One account-takeover edge case.** When Clerk tells the backend "a new user signed up", the backend hands over an old account with the same email *without checking the email was verified*. Clerk normally only sends verified emails, so this is probably not exploitable today — but it is a one-line check and it protects your own super-admin account.

### 6.4 Things to fix before colleagues use it (the "medium" list)

In plain words, roughly in order of importance:

* **The exam timer trusts the student's computer clock.** A clock that is a few minutes off shows the wrong countdown and produces confusing "time is up / already submitted" errors. Fix: the server sends its time.
* **Every autosave re-sends the entire quiz to the student.** It works, but it is the single biggest waste: about 90 % of the data an exam moves. This is what makes a 1,000-student exam exceed the free plan (see §8). Fix: split the query so only the answers refresh.
* **Nothing limits how often anything can be called.** Only join-code guessing is limited. A broken or hostile client could hammer saves, invites, the AI connector or the grading engine. Fix: per-user limits (the design is in the audit).
* **A huge or weird code submission can make grading time out, and then the system retries it every minute forever.** The same happens for a student whose account was deleted mid-task. Fix: cap page size, mark failed grading, show it to the lecturer.
* **The preview frame's protection against loading outside images can be bypassed** with specially written HTML. Nothing can run, and nothing can be stolen today, but a student could make a tracking pixel load when *you* open their work. Fix: build the preview with a real HTML parser and make the frame fully isolated.
* **The integrity colours are advice, not proof — and they can be unfair.** A hostile student can simply block the reporting and look perfectly green. An honest student gets points for Ctrl+F, an OS notification, a laptop going to sleep, or pressing Esc in fullscreen. Keep the honest position from the plan ("the system flags, the lecturer decides"), label the colour as client-reported, and soften the obvious false flags.
* **The AI connector could be tightened**: a short-lived internal credential is valid for 10 minutes and could be replayed if ever leaked; a retry after a lost response can create a duplicate quiz; there's no rate limit on agent calls; the join code is sent to the agent for no reason.
* **No security headers** on either website (clickjacking protection and friends). One hour of config.
* **Deleting or unpublishing leaves orphaned student work** in the database; deleting a user leaves dangling references. Needs rules and a retention job.
* **Task images are not pinned to your ImageKit account** (the setting was never set on either deployment).
* **No error reporting**: if something breaks for a student in production, you only find out if they tell you.
* **Two-factor sign-in for staff** needs checking in the Clerk dashboard.

### 6.5 Small stuff

Eighteen low-priority items: stale documentation (the plan file still describes repos and tables that don't exist, the studio says joining is "coming soon"), a lecturer's email showing to students when no name is set, a dead helper function, duplicated files, a leftover `.env` backup file on your disk that should be deleted and its secrets rotated, and similar hygiene. They're all listed in the audit.

## 7. What is *not* a problem (so you don't waste worry on it)

* **Money.** A full 1,000-student exam costs cents in backend usage. Vercel is almost unused. Clerk's free plan allows 50,000 monthly users.
* **Speed.** The backend handles the save rate of hundreds of students at once with room to spare, as long as the two fixes above (split query, caps on huge files) are done.
* **The stack.** Nothing found justifies changing Next.js, Vercel, Clerk or Convex.
* **The sandbox design.** Running checks in the browser for instant ticks and again on the server for grading is the right design; the server result is the only one that counts, so a student tampering with their browser gains nothing.

## 8. Money and limits, explained

You asked earlier for the real ceilings. Here they are, from the vendors' own pages today:

**Convex (the backend).**
* Free / Starter: 1 million function calls and 1 GB of database traffic per month, and — the important one — **at most 1,000 people connected at the same time** and 16 functions running at once. Free *without a card* simply **stops** when a quota is used up. Starter is the same plan *with a card*: overruns cost $2.20 per extra million calls and $0.22 per extra GB — cents.
* Pro: $25 per month, 25 million calls, 50 GB, **10,000 people connected at once**.
* What that means for you: today one 1,000-student exam would use about 100,000 calls (fine) and about 1.45 GB of traffic (over the free 1 GB because of the "re-send the whole quiz" waste). After the fix it's about 0.5 GB. The thing you cannot fix with code is the 1,000-connection cap: students plus lecturers plus you, all open tabs count. Practically, **about 900 people at the same time is the free-plan ceiling**; above that you need Pro.
* Advice: attach a card now (so a quota overrun during an exam costs cents instead of stopping the exam), and move to Pro when you plan anything near 800 simultaneous people.

**Clerk (sign-in).** 50,000 monthly users free. The one limit that matters: from a single IP address, only 5 sign-ins per 10 seconds. A classroom shares one IP, so 300 students signing in at the same moment would take about 10 minutes. The rule you already know is the right one: **everyone signs in before the exam starts**; sessions stay valid.

**Vercel.** You pay for Pro already; the apps barely use it.

**Email (when we add it).** Resend's free plan is 100 emails per *day*. One "new work" notice to a 300-student course already exceeds that. Budget $20/month for the Pro plan when email goes live.

**Registered vs active vs simultaneous.** 1,000 registered students and 50 lecturers cost nothing extra on any plan. What matters is how many have a Kalami tab open at the same moment (the connection cap) and, per exam, how much data each student moves (fixed by the split query).
